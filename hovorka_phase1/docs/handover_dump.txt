2. Project in plain language
The project investigates whether Model Predictive Control (MPC) can regulate blood glucose more effectively than a fixed/open-loop insulin strategy when a meal causes glucose to rise. The long-term application is an artificial pancreas: a closed-loop system that uses glucose measurements to decide how much insulin to deliver.
The present simulation compares three cases: (1) open-loop insulin control, (2) MPC without advance knowledge of a meal, and (3) MPC with meal preview. Meal preview means the controller is given information about an upcoming meal disturbance early enough to account for it in its prediction and insulin decision. The central research question is whether that extra information helps the controller respond earlier and reduce the post-meal glucose peak.
3. Essential concepts for the incoming teammate
Glucose (G) — The regulated variable. In the current results it is reported in mg/dL. The controller aims to keep it near a target/reference.
Insulin input (I or insulin command) — The manipulated input produced by the controller or fixed strategy. The logged maximum is a numerical command/output; its physical units and whether it represents rate, normalized units, or another quantity must be confirmed from the model.
Meal disturbance — A meal introduces glucose into the system and tends to raise blood glucose. In a simulation this is represented by a disturbance signal or meal-appearance model.
Open-loop control — The insulin input is prescribed rather than continually recalculated from measured glucose error.
MPC without meal preview — At each control update, MPC predicts future glucose using its model and chooses an input sequence, but does not receive advance knowledge of the future meal event.
MPC with meal preview — MPC receives advance meal information and can account for the expected disturbance in its prediction. This is intended to improve the timing of insulin delivery.
Prediction horizon — How far into the future the MPC predicts system behavior.
Control horizon — How many future control moves are optimized before the remaining moves are constrained/held according to the implementation.
Constraints and tuning — MPC typically balances tracking error against control effort while respecting input and possibly rate limits. Results depend on objective weights, horizons, sampling time, constraints, model accuracy, and disturbance timing.

6. What the numbers mean
Compared with open loop, the no-preview MPC peak is lower by 104.597 − 104.051 = 0.546 mg/dL. The meal-preview MPC peak is lower by 104.597 − 103.664 = 0.933 mg/dL. The preview case is also lower than the no-preview MPC case by 104.051 − 103.664 = 0.387 mg/dL.
The maximum insulin command rises from 7.000 in open loop to 7.754 with no-preview MPC and 7.884 with meal-preview MPC. In this particular simulation, the preview controller produces the lowest peak glucose, while also producing the largest logged insulin command. This is consistent with the idea that preview information can let the controller respond to an upcoming meal, but the differences in peak glucose are small.
Do not overstate this result. It does not prove that MPC is universally better, that the controller is clinically safe, or that meal preview will always help. It is evidence for these particular model settings, disturbance, and controller tuning. Before making strong claims, confirm that all three cases use comparable initial conditions, the same meal disturbance, the same simulation duration, and consistent output definitions.
7. How to describe the graphs verbally
The glucose comparison plot should show three glucose-versus-time curves. All cases are exposed to the meal-related disturbance. The open-loop curve reaches the highest documented peak, about 104.597 mg/dL. The no-preview MPC curve has a slightly lower peak of about 104.051 mg/dL. The meal-preview MPC curve has the lowest peak of the three, about 103.664 mg/dL. The curves' exact shapes, time of peak, time to return toward baseline, and any oscillations cannot be described reliably from the peak values alone; inspect the actual plotted traces before describing those features.
The insulin comparison plot should show the insulin command over time for the three cases. The recorded maxima are 7.000, 7.754, and 7.884 for open loop, no-preview MPC, and meal-preview MPC respectively. Do not infer the total insulin delivered or the area under the insulin curve from the maximum alone. If insulin usage is an important claim, calculate an appropriate integral or total-delivery metric using the correct signal units and time base.

11. Hovorka model: what it is and why it matters
The Hovorka model is a more detailed physiological model commonly used in artificial-pancreas research. Rather than representing glucose response as a very simple input/output relationship, it represents important processes such as subcutaneous insulin absorption, insulin action in the body, glucose distribution/transport, endogenous glucose production and utilization, and meal glucose appearance. Exact state definitions and equations depend on the specific published formulation and implementation chosen.
The Hovorka model should be introduced as a separate, carefully verified plant model—not by simply renaming the current simulation. The existing MPC comparison can remain a useful baseline. A new Hovorka implementation should first be validated without MPC, then connected to the controller after the plant behaves plausibly.
12. Suggested Hovorka implementation roadmap
Choose and document one reference formulation: Obtain the original paper or a reliable published implementation. Record the exact equations, state meanings, units, parameter values, and any patient-specific parameters. Do not mix equations or parameters from different implementations without documenting the changes.
Create a parameter/units sheet: For every parameter, record symbol, meaning, value, units, source, and whether it is fixed or individualized. Verify unit conversions explicitly.
Implement the plant first: Build the differential equations in MATLAB Function blocks, Simulink integrators, or a MATLAB ODE function. Keep meal input and insulin input separate. Use clear names for states and outputs.
Check initial conditions and basal steady state: With no meal and basal insulin, verify that the model remains near its expected basal operating point. If it drifts unexpectedly, check equations, units, parameter values, initial conditions, and solver settings.
Test meal response open loop: Apply a documented meal profile with a known time and magnitude. Confirm that glucose responds in the expected direction and with plausible timing. Compare against the reference formulation or published benchmark where possible.
Test insulin response separately: Apply a small, controlled insulin change and inspect whether glucose responds in the expected direction and on a plausible timescale. Check for numerical instability or unrealistic negative states.
Add logging and unit tests: Log glucose, insulin, meal appearance, and relevant model states. Add checks for NaN/Inf values, negative states where physiologically impossible, and expected basal behavior.
Connect MPC only after plant validation: Define the controller's prediction model, sampling time, input constraints, target, horizons, and objective. Confirm that the model used for prediction is compatible with the Hovorka plant and the controller's update rate.
Repeat all three scenarios: Run open loop, MPC without meal preview, and MPC with meal preview using identical initial conditions and meal disturbance. Compare the same metrics as the current model.
Assess robustness: Test different meal sizes/timings, parameter variation, sensor noise or delay, and actuator constraints as appropriate to the project scope. Report limitations honestly.
13. Important caution about the artificial-pancreas framing
This is a simulation and control-engineering project, not a medical device. A lower simulated peak does not establish clinical safety. A serious evaluation should consider hypoglycemia risk, time in range, insulin constraints, sensor and actuator delays, parameter uncertainty, and failure cases. Do not use this model or its controller to make real insulin dosing decisions.
